Sometimes I feel like I've been putting so much time into developing my website that the content is an afterthought. It's very typical of me to overthink the design and flow of whatever I'm working on, and subsequently forget that I'm supposed to be documenting my thoughts, lol.

###### neocities nostalgia, obsidian optimization

I got sucked into a Neocities rabbit hole over the past week while gathering inspiration for the my site. I wasn't around for the Geocities era, but Neocities reminds me of old Tumblr with all the pixel art and webrings. My own Tumblr was never much to look at, I had somewhat of a curatorial eye for whatever I reblogged, but my themes were pretty unoriginal and copied from whoever made free minimalist themes.

This site is spun up through Quartz + GitHub Pages. I write notes in my [[Obsidian]] vault and use the Quartz Syncer tool to push my changes through to GitHub. I chose to use Quartz and GitHub Pages since I didn't want to pay for hosting and wanted to [[indie web|own my data]]. I was introduced to Obsidian through some TikToks on [[digital gardening]] (see Maggie Appleton's essay on digital gardens [here](https://maggieappleton.com/garden-history), which seems to be THE document of record on the topic.) I like the ideal of digital gardening in theory, but the non-linear, non-hierarchical structure is a bit overwhelming...

A quick scan of other digital gardens leads to a lot of overlap between productivity-guru bullshit and heavy networking/AI content. None of this particularly interests me, and that degree of optimization of my personal life feels unnecessary for what I have going on. I'm mostly here to identify patterns between what floats around in my head. I like the cozier touches of personal websites, but the visual aspect of my interconnected notes is interesting, too. I think I may have analysis paralysis because I want too many things at once, although the customizability of Quartz means I could probably build exactly what I want if I put in the work.

###### getting started is the hardest part

I'm reminded of this quote from John Swartzwelder's interview in [The New Yorker](https://www.newyorker.com/culture/the-new-yorker-interview/john-swartzwelder-sage-of-the-simpsons):

> [!quote] “Since writing is very hard and rewriting is comparatively easy and rather fun, I always write my scripts all the way through as fast as I can, the first day, if possible, putting in crap jokes and pattern dialogue—“Homer, I don’t want you to do that.” “Then I won’t do it.” Then the next day, when I get up, the script’s been written. It’s lousy, but it’s a script. The hard part is done. It’s like a crappy little elf has snuck into my office and badly done all my work for me, and then left with a tip of his crappy hat. All I have to do from that point on is fix it. So I’ve taken a very hard job, writing, and turned it into an easy one, rewriting, overnight.”

This idea struck me as a novel way to tackle the most challenging parts of a project head-on, and fleshing out the minutiae later. Not that I'm doing much long-form writing these days, but publishing my notes on the internet has led me to become slightly more intentional with how I'm structuring my [[My Thoughts on Dreaming Spanish After Reaching Level 6|words]]. However, I don't want the thoughts of _how_ I'm going to organize my work to cloud the actual writing process. Just write first and worry about linking and tagging and folders later.
