I recently finished _Filterworld: How Algorithms Flattened Culture_ by Kyle Chayka. Although repetitive at times, the book made me re-evaluate my relationship with recommendation algorithms and how they've warped my personal sense of taste.

###### algorithmic anxiety & working in digital marketing

I thought Chayka did a great job defining the sensation of algorithmic anxiety, where we feel subject to these mysterious, invisible forces that shape where we travel, what music we listen to, what we eat. While nothing in the book was a bombshell revelation, I agree that I've felt a certain flattening of culture in response to the proliferation of recommendation algorithms. I enjoy listening to music and finding new artists, but I've hopelessly thrown on a curated, personalized Spotify playlist plenty of times when I just want to fill the silence on a drive. I wouldn't call this experience personally satisfying, the music is oftentimes not that interesting or what I really _want_ to listen to in the moment.

I have to admit that I feel a certain level of culpability in this whole ordeal since I've chosen to work in digital marketing. I've worked to implement pixels, set up enhanced conversion APIs, tracked clicks, all in the name of selling slightly bullshit products and services. There's a part of me that truly enjoys the systems-oriented operation behind capturing and selling attention (I'm specifically referring to performance marketing here), but I think that also explains why I feel so exasperated and conflicted when it comes to my own feeds. I don't go on Facebook and rarely explore Instagram beyond the confines of my private account. However, I love Twitter and can't get myself to leave even as the enshittification worsens and my feed is clogged by AI engagement accounts. I have firsthand knowledge of how the data I willingly volunteer is used against me, and I still haven't quit.

###### the importance of taste & curation

Earlier this year, I read some [pieces](https://www.theguardian.com/media/2026/jun/14/have-i-been-influenced-personal-taste-out-of-fashion-algorithm) insisting how personal taste was the final differentiator for companies and products in the AI era. (I realize now that I definitely read this [article](https://www.newyorker.com/culture/infinite-scroll/why-tech-bros-are-now-obsessed-with-taste), also by Kyle Chayka.) I can understand this perspective, and I can probably be perceived through the lens of the media I consume. Algorithms have directed me towards the music, TV shows and books that have shaped me into who I am.

Towards the end of _Filterworld_, Chayka emphasizes the importance of connoisseurs and curators in culture, figures who can shape and refine the context in which we engage with art and view the world. If you've known me for a while, you'll know I was a student DJ for my college's radio station. Somewhat embarrassingly, this is something I'm really proud of. It was a surprisingly competitive role to get, there was a giant stack of applications in the office from people who never got a call back. After creating a sample playlist and an interview to make sure you knew enough about your chosen artist's music and influences (or could bullshit your way through it), you then moved onto an 8-week training. After the training period ended, you were off and free to curate whatever you wished, as long as it fell within the confines of the FCC guidelines.

I remember feeling intense imposter syndrome when it came to creating my show each week, and I'm 100% aware that I projected it on myself. I looked up to my peers at the station as people who had such fully formed, cultured views on music. They seemed to possess a high-level, distinctive taste and music knowledge that I could only pretend to have. Looking back, this is incredibly silly. I was surrounded by people who were invigorated by music discovery, and I had access to shelves and shelves of CDs and vinyls that were sent to the station. I'd hang around the station multiple times a week, poring over a CD's liner notes and immersing myself in the visual world of a record. This was impactful for me to try to understand the album as a body of work, in the way the artist intended. I prided myself on doing the work to create an engaging, cohesive show. I didn't even care if anyone listened or not, I was just thrilled that my work was being broadcast on the airwaves at all. This all took time, focus, and an interest to dig deeper. I don't fault anyone who just goes to Spotify and cues up Daily Mix 1.

###### moving forward

After graduating and leaving the station, the sense of duty I felt towards diving deeper and creating an engaging playlist went away. I stopped regularly reading music blogs and listening to other shows. I get a lot of recommendations from Twitter now. I searched for Sophia Stel's album after remembering a few joke tweets about it. I've spent the last week listening to Built to Spill, all because I saw a screenshot of Carry the Zero on my feed. I first came across Geese on Reddit during the 3D Country era, so I guess that counts as another algorithm (and the [music marketing machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geese_psy-op_allegations)...) at work?

The first step I see to re-engage myself is to listen and learn more about the shit that I do like. The second step would be sharing the things that I like. I've dabbled in expressing my interests online for ~20 years now (other blogs, radio, etc) and I think the process of sitting down to think about my interests allows me think more critically and crystallize my thoughts. Creating the site and poking around other personal blogs has been such a lovely time, there are still pockets on the internet that feel wholly unique. This is partially why the indie web interests me, I like the idea of owning my data, making my website a fun place to be, and free from any algorithmic influence.
