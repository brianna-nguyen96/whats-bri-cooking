Recipe by Eric Kim from NYT Cooking

![[Attachments/984528A1-4D68-48B3-9835-7962646E4B0B_1_105_c.jpeg|center|363]]I didn't have any plans to roast a chicken this week, but Sprouts had this whole chicken on sale for \$1.69/lb on the day that I popped in. Even the cashier commented that it was a good deal! For some reason I feel so pleased whenever cashiers comment on my thrifty spending.

To be honest, I hadn't seen this recipe before I searched for "roast chicken" in the NYT Cooking app. Eric Kim has some of my favorite recipes in NYT Cooking. The [sheet-pan bibimbap](https://cooking.nytimes.com/recipes/1022131-sheet-pan-bibimbap?smid=share-url) and [gochugaru salmon](https://cooking.nytimes.com/recipes/1022132-gochugaru-salmon-with-crispy-rice?smid=share-url) are in steady rotation in my kitchen. With that being said, I don't consider NYT Cooking to be a paragon of authenticity when it comes to Asian cooking. I was intrigued by this recipe's phở gà influence (although it's not my favorite type of phở - I prefer beef) and wanted to see just how "Vietnamese-inspired" this chicken truly was.

Luckily I had (almost) all of the ingredients at home already. I subbed star anise for fennel seeds, since it seemed like a waste to go buy fennel seeds just to use half a teaspoon. After spreading the seasoning blend over the chicken, I let it brine for 7 hours in the fridge. I took it out an hour before cooking to bring the chicken to room temp, and tossed in some more anise and cinnamon sticks before throwing it in the oven.

I'm still adjusting to the idiosyncrasies of my parents' oven (please refer to the [[Slow-Roasted Chicken with Buttered Tomatoes]]), so while the recipe calls for setting the oven to 400 degrees, I set it to...390. Not sure if this made a difference? In one of two mistakes, I forgot to add the required 1/4 cup of water into the pan until 15 minutes after the chicken had already started cooking. Eric claims that the water leads to "beautifully rendered chicken fat, evenly bronzed skin and the tenderest pull-apart meat". I'd never heard of this before, but sure, I'm all in. Secondly, I didn't start basting the chicken until 45 minutes into the cook time. The recipe doesn't specify which intervals to baste, but it doesn't seem like an exact science. Either way, the distinct phở gà aroma began to waft through the kitchen. It may have been because I added the extra anise and cinnamon, but I 100% see the Vietnamese influence here. It smelled INCREDIBLE.

When basting the chicken at the hour mark, it had this gorgeous golden sheen, almost like a cartoon roast chicken. At the 70-minute mark, the chicken began to swing towards the "toastier" end of golden, so 65 minutes would be a perfect time to take the chicken out in the future. Once again, I still haven't nailed down the oven...

The chicken tasted great! To my surprise, the dry brine with anise, cinnamon, clove and coriander penetrated the chicken very well. Chris and I just ate the chicken over rice, but if I was serving this at a dinner party I'd include the various phở accoutrements - sriracha, Thai basil, lime, etc. The breast was incredibly tender, much more than with the slow-roasted chicken. The leftovers the next day were just as good, and the ~5 pound chicken was plenty for 4+ servings. 100% would make this again.
